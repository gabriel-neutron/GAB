## Summary

**The problem.** Since 2022, the European Union, the United States, the United Kingdom and their allies have imposed wide-ranging sanctions on Russia, from oil and gas to military technology. Yet the war goes on, and it costs more and more: at constant prices, the defence budget planned for 2025 is 2.6 times the spending of 2021. Where do the money and the technology that let Russia fight come from?

**The method.** The authors built a database of more than 5,300 entities, where every fact points to an archived document. In it, the report looks for the nodes, that is the companies, ports, ships, banks and payment channels through which the money and the goods of the war move, that are exposed to sanctions, active in 2024-2026 and tied to a country that does not apply them.

**The result.** Sanctions make the war more costly for Russia, but they do not cut its money or its goods: trade moves to partners that do not apply the sanctions, and to a parallel market of tankers, payment relays and companies with no documented owner.

**What the report measures.**

- –**Designations:** When the EU and the United States designate the same tanker, the United States does so first in more than eight cases out of ten, and the median gap from the first to the second regime is 87 days.

- –**Port calls:** Of 711 tankers with a dated designation, 318 called at a port at least once after it, and 22 of them called in the EU or the United Kingdom.

- –**Owners and managers:** In the private fleet and in oil trading, the ownership chains often end at a company with no documented owner: according to the sanctions lists and, for part of them, to Ukrainian military intelligence (GUR), a party to the conflict, sixteen shipowners or managers each own or operate at least six designated vessels with no documented ultimate owner. On the archived pages of GUR, 427 of 757 ships that give both dates show an owner or manager that started after the designation.

**What the sources show.**

- –**Customers:** From 2021 to 2025, EU purchases from Russia fell by 84%;[^1] China became Russia's first customer, and India bought twenty times as much Russian crude by weight.

- –**Oil:** According to the KSE Institute, the tankers of the so-called "shadow fleet", tied to a country outside the coalition and with no Western insurance, carried 79% of the crude exported by sea in January 2026.[^2] From August 2023 to October 2025, Urals sold $10 to $16 below Brent each month; since November 2025, after the OFAC designation of Rosneft and Lukoil, it has sold $19 to $27 below.

- –**Budget:** The share of oil and gas in federal revenue falls from 51% in 2014 to 18.5% in January–September 2026, partly because that revenue is shrinking.[^3]

- –**Currency:** The rouble settled 53% of Russian exports in 2025, against 14% in 2021,[^4] and, according to the Russian government, the rouble and the yuan settle almost all payments with China.

- –**Components:** Of the priority goods for weapons that its partners declared as sold to Russia in 2025, China supplied 77% and Hong Kong 11%,[^5] and parts made after the invasion turned up in Russian weapons in 2023.

The sources of the other figures are given in the body of the report.

## Introduction

Sanctions against Russia did not begin in 2022. As early as 2014, when Russia annexed Crimea, the European Union froze the assets of 21 Russian and Crimean officials. It then barred the five largest Russian state banks from raising debt on its markets with a maturity above 90 days. It also restricted sales of oil technology and of dual-use goods meant for the army.[^6] Later, in December 2016, the United States kept its sanctions in place until Russia implemented the Minsk agreements, signed in 2014 and 2015 to stop the fighting in eastern Ukraine.[^7] By contrast, after the invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022, the measures changed scale: an embargo on Russian oil, a price cap on Russian oil sold elsewhere, and bans on ports and banks (figure 1).

Yet the war goes on in 2026, and the Russian state manages to pay for it. At constant prices, the defence budget planned for 2025 is 2.6 times the spending of 2021,[^8] while over the same period oil and gas revenue falls by 36%.[^9] If that revenue shrinks while war spending rises, which channels carry the money and the goods of the war?

Which nodes (companies, infrastructure, ships, banks) make it possible to finance the war? Who owns them? How do they get around the sanctions?

The report shows which routes stay open after each measure; open sources rarely show circumvention itself, so it gives each mechanism as a claim of its source or as a hypothesis.

This report finds that sanctions make the war more costly for Russia, but they do not cut its money or its goods: trade moves to partners that do not apply the sanctions, and to a parallel market of tankers, payment relays and companies with no documented owner.

## 1 Method

To wage a long war, Russia needs two flows: the foreign currency that its exports bring in, and the imported goods that its factories need to make weapons. The report therefore does not start from national totals. It starts from the points through which this money and these goods must pass.

### 1.1 The three layers

The report therefore divides the Russian economy into nodes: sites (a port, a factory, a gas field), companies, fleets of ships or payment channels (a bank, a payment agent, or a token such as A7A5). Each node serves one of three functions, which the report calls layers:

- –**Pay:** Earn the foreign currency that pays for the war, mainly by selling oil and gas;

- –**Finance:** Move that money, through banks and payment channels;

- –**Fight:** Import the components and make the weapons.

These three layers follow the path of the money (figure 2). The foreign-currency revenue of the pay layer settles the imports of the fight layer, and this settlement passes through the finance layer. The money also takes a second, domestic path: taxes on oil and gas fill the federal budget, which orders weapons from Russian factories.

### 1.2 Nodes under watch

A node is under watch when it meets three conditions; this is a filter, not a ranking:

- –**Exposed to sanctions:** The European Union (EU), the American Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) or the United Kingdom designates it, or a designated person owns 50% or more of it;

- –**Active in 2024-2026:** A dated production, transit or new designation shows it;


- –**Tied to a third country:** Its head office, the flag of its ships or a counterparty is in a country that does not apply the sanctions of the coalition (the EU, the United States, the United Kingdom and their allies). An oil exporter meets it through its buyer.

These conditions single out the nodes where the sanctions are in place but the activity goes on with a third country: where circumvention may be at work, and where a new measure could have an effect.

The choice of period follows the calendar of the sanctions. By 2024, the major oil measures are in place: the embargo, the price cap, and then the first designations of shipowners that do not respect that cap.[^11] The years 2024-2026 therefore show how trade has adapted to these sanctions. After each measure, the report looks for correlations between the sanctions and trade, and then proposes explanations, each one attributed to its source, or presented as a hypothesis when no source establishes it.

### 1.3 The data and its control

The authors built a database of more than 5,300 entities (such as companies, ships, sites, ports, banks, people and legal acts) and of the relations between them, such as a designation, an ownership, the management of a ship, a port call, an oil flow or a payment. These facts are taken from the official sanctions lists, company registers and published investigations, and each one points to the archived document that establishes it. The reliability of each official source follows the ADMIRALTY scale (Annex A.2), which rates the issuer with a letter, from A for an official issuer (sanctions list, company register, official journal) to F when the issuer cannot be judged.

In total, 451 nodes are under watch, counting the ships of one management company as a single node. The pay layer holds the most, because the lists of designated ships are long and well documented. For 336 of them, each of the three conditions rests on a document from an official issuer. Annex A describes how the facts are collected and checked. Section A.3 says how the count of nodes under watch was made.

## 2 Pay

Oil and gas carry the Russian economy. They are the most discussed topic and often the worst understood. In 2021, their rent (what they earn beyond their cost of production) was worth 15.5% of GDP;[^12] fuels made up more than 40% of goods exports,[^13] and oil and gas brought in more than a third of federal budget revenue.[^14] If sanctions were to dry up the money of the war, this is where they should bite first.

### 2.1 The buyers

From 2022, the EU almost closed its market to Russian oil, with an embargo on crude carried by sea, then on oil products.[^15] It also cut its purchases of Russian gas, then decided in January 2026 to stop them in stages.[^16] By contrast, Asia took over (figure 3): China became Russia's first customer, and India bought twenty times as much Russian crude by weight in 2025 as in 2021.[^17] Two reasons explain this shift:[^18]

- –**Price:** With fewer buyers allowed to take it, and more risk for those who do, Russian crude must be sold at a discount, so it is cheaper for the buyers that stay;

- –**Politics:** China and India do not apply the sanctions, and their leaders do not want to look as if they yield to the United States.

Gas follows the same path to Asia: in 2025, for the first time, Gazprom delivered more gas by pipeline to China than to Europe.[^19] But China sets the terms. To sell the gas of the Yamal peninsula, which Europe no longer buys, Russia has only one possible customer. That customer is in no hurry to seal a deal on the new

### 2.2 The price and the cap

To measure what Russia loses on each barrel, the report compares its oil with Brent, the North Sea oil that serves as the reference price. Urals, the main Russian crude exported, is heavier and higher in sulphur, so before the war it usually sold a little below Brent. In 2019, this price gap, called the discount, averaged $0.73 a barrel for Urals loaded in the Baltic, from January to early October.[^22] Since December 2022, an EU company or a company of its allies can no longer carry, insure or finance Russian crude sold above a price cap (figure 4).[^23] The coalition chose a cap rather than a full embargo because it pursues two aims at once: reducing the oil profits that let Russia wage the war, and keeping world energy markets stable and well supplied.[^24]

## 4 Fight

No modern army can do without on-board electronics. A Russian cruise missile or attack drone contains chips, sensors and engines that Russia does not make, or does not make in sufficient numbers. Oil money must therefore buy these parts abroad, even though the coalition has closed its own markets. What remains to be seen is who now sells these goods, at what price, and by which routes they reach the arms factories.

### 4.1 The suppliers

Since 2022, the coalition no longer sells to Russia either its military technologies or its dual-use technologies, that is, civilian products, such as chips, that the Russian army can use.[^80] Those most useful to its weapons are gathered in a shared list called CHP (Common High Priority), such as integrated circuits or computer numerical control machine tools.[^81] The report measures these sales through the declarations of the selling countries.

China and Hong Kong take the place of the coalition (figure 11), but in two different roles. China is above all a producer: for most of the dual-use technologies it sells to Russia, its exports rise without its imports rising, and re-export affects only a dozen high-technology products.[^82] Targeting China therefore hits production.

Hong Kong plays another role, that of a platform. It takes a rising share of Russian imports of processors and integrated circuits, in place of the coalition, which may signal re-exported Western chips.[^83] Hong Kong applies only the sanctions decided by the UN Security Council, which do not target Russia: EU and US sanctions are not part of its law, although US export controls still follow goods of US origin. This financial centre also lets a company hide its owner, and lets new companies be created or dissolved quickly. It is therefore a convenient place of transit

Russia's neighbours can also serve as a route. ## 5 Who owns what

Behind every port, every tanker and every bank stands an owner: sometimes the state, sometimes a person close to power, sometimes nobody who can be named. Who holds these nodes, and how far does a sanction reach into them?

A sanction does not hit only the companies it names. In the United States, a company owned 50% or more, in aggregate, by designated persons is blocked as well.[^95] The EU applies a test of 50% or more, in aggregate, and the United Kingdom a test of more than 50% that adds control.[^96] Knowing who owns a company therefore tells how far a sanction reaches it. The report traces each chain of shares up to the state, to a person, or to a company with no documented ultimate owner (figure 12).

### 5.1 The state and its holdings

According to the latest disclosures, at the top, the state holds the large companies through a few holdings. ## 6 Limitations

Before drawing conclusions, the report must say what the facts gathered here cannot establish.

### 6.1 Limits of the evidence

The report does not measure the effect of sanctions: where no source establishes a causal link, that link remains a hypothesis. Four further limits bear on the facts themselves:

- –**Coverage of the database:** It covers maritime transport well, but banks and armaments poorly. It links few companies to a payment or to a component, and it gives an amount for almost none of them; the section on payments therefore rests on fewer facts than the section on the fleet.

- –**Reliability of sources:** The letter rates the issuer, not the truth of each claim. The report shows it only for official issuers: the letters of the press and of think tanks are not shown, so the reader cannot weigh their facts.

- –**Mirror data:** Because Russia no longer publishes its trade by product, the report reads the declarations of its partners, which therefore cover only part of the trade. The United Arab Emirates and Vietnam report no product-level data to UN Comtrade for 2024-2025, and Kazakhstan and Thailand report data only up to February 2024 and February 2025.[^127]

- –**The fleet:** Changes of owner and manager come from the GUR, a party to the conflict. It gives the vessel's current owner or manager and the date it took up the role, but rarely the first change after designation.[^128] The loss of the AIS identity tied to the IMO number, which the Global Fishing Watch registry makes visible, remains a lead: the record has archived the vessel identities of that registry since 28 October 2026, but the report does not measure that loss.

Finally, the authors read and reviewed the whole report, but the facts of the database are checked by rules and by AI agents, not one by one by a person (Annex A). As a result, each fact must be checked at its source before it is used for a designation.

### 6.2 Research leads

These limits point to directions for research:

- –**Measure the effect of sanctions:** Compare monthly series of prices and export volumes with the dates of designation, to test whether a designation really widens the discount on Russian crude.

- –**Document payments and components:** Link the companies of the database to payments and components, with amounts, using the alerts of the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), US Treasury press releases and teardown studies of Russian weapons, to learn what volume passes through Kyrgyz banks and A7's sub-agents.

- –**Complete the mirror data:** Look for customs data that cover the United Arab Emirates, Vietnam, Kazakhstan and Thailand, to learn what share of the priority goods for weapons passes through these countries.

- –**Date the changes in the fleet:** Archive the history of AIS identities and compare it with the flag registers, to learn how long a designated vessel takes to change identity.

## Conclusion

Sanctions weigh on the Russian economy, but they do not stop it. The revenue the state draws from oil and gas is falling, while it raises its other taxes to pay for an ever costlier defence. The discount on Russian crude widened after the American sanctions of 2025, and the goods Russia must import cost more. The war therefore costs more to wage, but it can still be financed.

Russia adapts in two ways. First, it relies on a parallel market on the margins of Western sanctions: tankers outside the coalition, managers based in third countries, payment agents, and shell companies created and dissolved quickly. Second, and legally, it turns to partners that do not apply the sanctions. China and India buy its oil, and China sells it most of the priority goods for weapons. These partners do not provide this service for nothing: Russia has few other choices, and they pay less for its oil and charge more for their goods. Part of the cost of sanctions for Russia thus becomes, according to the report's hypothesis, a gain for its new partners.

EU sanctions follow this shift step by step. They go up the chain: the product, then the means, then the source. The EU first targeted oil, through the embargo and the price cap, then the vessels that carry it, then the ports it leaves from. Each step answers the circumvention of the one before and arrives late; the report assumes that the parallel market re-forms faster than the lists grow.

## Recommendations

- –A sanctions authority of the EU or the United Kingdom can use each new OFAC tanker designation as an early signal for its own review, because OFAC lists a shared tanker first in more than eight cases out of ten.

- –A sanctions authority of the EU or the United Kingdom can check the port calls in its own ports of the 22 designated tankers that called there after their designation.

- –An authority that lists ships can check, at each update, the current owner and manager of the ships it has already listed, because, on the pages of Ukrainian military intelligence (GUR), a party to the conflict, 427 of 757 ships show an owner or manager that started after the designation.

- –Where no source names the ultimate owner, an authority can designate each company for its activity and attach each new manager or trader to the group it replaces, as the Council of the EU did for Citrine Marine and Nord Axis.

- –A researcher can complete the mirror data with customs data for the United Arab Emirates, Vietnam, Kazakhstan and Thailand, which report no product data or only part of it to UN Comtrade for 2024-2025.

- –A funder can pay for the archiving of AIS identity histories and for a check of the fleet facts by a person, because the report's fleet findings rest on one source that is a party to the conflict and on a registry that is not archived, and no person has checked each fact of the database against its source.

## Annex A Research method

### A.1 The database

The report rests on a database of entities (such as companies, vessels, sites, ports, banks, persons, legal acts) and of the relations between them, such as a designation, a shareholding, the management of a vessel, a port call, an oil flow or a bank settlement. Each fact in the database points to an archived document (web page, official act, data file, register response), with the page and the excerpt that establish it. On 28 October 2026, the database holds 5,366 entities in its public data release (data-2026-10-28). The count of nodes under watch runs on this release. A legal act or an event is no node, so the count tests 4,930 entities.

These documents come from the official sanctions lists (OFAC, United Kingdom, EU acts), from company registers, from public statistics (UN Comtrade, Eurostat, World Bank, Bank of Russia, Russian Ministry of Finance), from research institutes (KSE, CREA, Bruegel) and from investigative press. Leads came from web and news searches (SearXNG, GDELT) and from the registers; a search result was never cited, and the queries were not logged. The documents of the data release were retrieved between 7 and 28 October 2026; the research closed on 20 November 2026.

Each document is stored as received, under its SHA-256 hash, which also forms its document id. The database records the address and the date of retrieval, with no time of day. The raw files are kept in a private store that cannot change or delete them; they are not published, and no web archive copy is recorded.

### A.2 Source rules

- –A designation is cited from the official line of the OFAC list, of the UK list or of the EU act.

- –The portal of Ukrainian military intelligence (GUR) is cited sheet by sheet, and its claims remain attributed to the GUR, a party to the conflict.

- –Because Russia has not published its trade by product since 2022, that trade is read in the declarations of its partners (mirror data).

- –No data that comes from a leak is cited, but a press article about a leak may be.

- –A source that is known but not archived remains a lead and does not count as a fact; likewise, a value without a source remains undocumented.

- –The reliability of the issuer of each source follows a scale adapted from the NATO grading system, also called the ADMIRALTY scale, from A (an official issuer on its own record) to F (cannot be judged);[^129] a party to the conflict is rated C at most. A separate digit from 1 to 6 rates each fact by the number of independent issuers that give it. The report shows the letter of official issuers only; the data release gives the letter and the digit of each claim that has both.

- –Each source has a level set from its terms of use, which were read. A source whose terms forbid reuse stays internal: a fact can cite it, but its data are not republished.

### A.3 Selecting the nodes under watch

The three conditions of the Method section apply in the same way to all entities in the database:

- –**Exposed to sanctions:** A designation relation to an EU, OFAC or UK list, or a holding of 50% or more by a designated entity;

- –**Active in 2024-2026:** A relation dated in that period (designation, port call, transshipment, event);

- –**Tied to a third country:** The entity's country of registration, any flag of its vessels that the release gives, or the country of a direct counterparty is a country other than Russia that does not apply the coalition's sanctions. A country read only from a company's name or address does not count.

Vessels of one management company, or failing that of one owner, form a single node. One act of designation can meet more than one condition; where no activity is observed, a new designation stands as a sign of activity: it dates an act of enforcement, not an observed flow.

The count of nodes under watch is 451. It is the result of the script tools/critical-nodes-replay.ts on the data release data-2026-10-28. The command pnpm critical:replay downloads that release, checks its SHA-256 and gives the same count. A node that the release does not hold is not counted. A date that the release gives as a claim of a relation is a date of that relation. A designation with no date takes the date of its act. Russia is the target of the sanctions, so it is not a third country. For 336 of the 451 nodes, each condition rests on a document of an official issuer (ADMIRALTY letter A). The script derives the layer of a node from its entities, because the release gives no layer: Pay 399, Finance 5, Fight 47. The count changes with the rule: 605 nodes if Russia counts as a third country, 439 with no country of a counterparty. An earlier count of 493 (450 nodes of the record and 43 of a first list) and the figure of 416 cannot be rebuilt, because their query was not kept. Port calls carry no date, so most nodes are active only by a dated designation. The script and its command keep the older name "critical".

### A.4 Checking facts and text

The authors read and reviewed the whole report. AI was used for four jobs: to tag the sources, to run the verification and cross-check loops, to assist the drafting and the translation, and to search for blind spots. AI agents collect and archive the sources, then propose each fact with the excerpt of its source. Other agents, which start with a fresh context and did not propose that fact, check that the excerpt appears in the archived source and supports the fact. A failed check does not block the acceptance of a fact. Other facts still wait for a check or a better source. The few facts entered by hand carry a label that sets them apart from the facts produced by the agents. The counts, shares and medians are the authors' calculations, which no review agent validates one by one. Each part of the text was reviewed by agents distinct from its writer, and those agents checked its sources one by one. The authors did not check each fact of the database against its source.

### A.5 Ethics, security and independence

- –**Persons:** A natural person appears in the data release only when a sanctions act designates that person. No personal address, telephone number or e-mail is collected. Extracts of company registers and court files name legal persons only.

- –**Contact:** No subject was contacted, and no right of reply was offered before publication, because the report brings together public information only. Each page and each data file gives an address to report an error and an address for a right of reply.

- –**AI:** Language models were used for four tasks only: tagging the sources, the verification loops, help with drafting and translation, and the search for blind spots. Their providers read the full text of the stored documents.

- –**Independence:** The authors are solely responsible for the content. Claims of the GUR remain attributed to it.
