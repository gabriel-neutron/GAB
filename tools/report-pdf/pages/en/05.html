The nodes that sustain Russia’s war in 2026Introduction

Introduction

Sanctions against Russia did not begin in 2022. As early as 2014, when Russia annexed Crimea, the European Union froze the assets of 21 Russian and Crimean officials. It then barred the five largest Russian state banks from raising debt on its markets with a maturity above 90 days. It also restricted sales of oil technology and of dual-use goods meant for the army.6 Later, in December 2016, the United States kept its sanctions in place until Russia implemented the Minsk agreements, signed in 2014 and 2015 to stop the fighting in eastern Ukraine.7 By contrast, after the invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022, the measures changed scale: an embargo on Russian oil, a price cap on Russian oil sold elsewhere, and bans on ports and banks (figure 1).

Yet the war goes on in 2026, and the Russian state manages to pay for it. At constant prices, the defence budget planned for 2025 is 2.6 times the spending of 2021,8 while over the same period oil and gas revenue falls by 36%.9 If that revenue shrinks while war spending rises, which channels carry the money and the goods of the war?

Research question

Which nodes (companies, ports, fleets, banks) make it possible to finance the war? Who owns them? How do they get around the sanctions?
